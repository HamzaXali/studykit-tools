What do I need on the final?

By Ali Hamza

Last updated 6 November 2026

If your current work is already a percentage, and the final is a known share of the course, you can calculate the final score that hits a target. The arithmetic is short. The mistakes are usually in the numbers you feed it.

The answer is only as good as the weight in the syllabus and the current grade in the gradebook. This guide uses the same formula as the final grade calculator, and it says when that formula does not apply.

The formula

Call the current grade C, the final’s weight W (as a percent of the whole course), and the target course grade T. All three are percentages. The weight as a decimal is w = W ÷ 100. The score you need on the final is:

(T − C × (1 − w)) ÷ w

In words: take the part of the course that is already finished, see how many points that part contributes, subtract that contribution from the target, and divide what is left by the final’s share. If the final is the entire course, W is 100 and the score you need is simply the target.

The calculator rounds the required score up to two decimal places, so the figure it shows is enough rather than a hair short. If the required score is 0 or below, you are already at the target even with nothing on the final. If it is above 100, a final marked out of 100 cannot reach the target. In that case the tool also reports the best course grade a perfect final could give: C × (1 − w) + 100 × w.

Worked examples

Example 1. Your current grade is 80%, the final is worth 40% of the course, and you want an overall 70%. Then w = 0.40. Required score = (70 − 80 × 0.60) ÷ 0.40 = (70 − 48) ÷ 0.40 = 55. You need 55% on the final. That is exact, so rounding up does not change it.

Example 2. Your current grade is 84%, the final is worth 30%, and you want 90% overall. Required score = (90 − 84 × 0.70) ÷ 0.30 = (90 − 58.8) ÷ 0.30 = 104. A final marked out of 100 cannot produce 104. The best overall result with 100% on the final is 84 × 0.70 + 100 × 0.30 = 88.8%. The honest conclusion is that 90% is out of reach unless the syllabus allows something this formula does not include, such as extra credit or a replaced coursework mark.

Example 3. A final worth 25% scored at 76%, with a current grade of 68%, gives 68 × 0.75 + 76 × 0.25 = 70 as the course grade. The calculator’s second section does that reverse check.

Where the inputs go wrong

The current grade must leave the final out. If the gradebook already shows a course total that assumes a zero on the final, or that includes a predicted final, do not type that total into C. You would be counting the final twice or treating a guess as fact. If you are unsure, add up the completed items yourself using the weights in the syllabus, and ignore the final row.

Weights must describe the same course. A final that is “40% of the exam” is not the same as a final that is “40% of the module” if the exam itself is only part of the module. Convert to a share of the whole course before you calculate. Two assessments worth 20% and 30% are not a 50% final unless you sit them as one combined mark. If they are separate, this single-final formula is the wrong tool. Work through each component, or use the percentage and marks calculator when you are turning raw marks into a percentage first.

Letter targets are not universal. The buttons on the final grade calculator offer 90, 80, 70 and 60 as common US-style cutoffs for A, B, C and D. Your syllabus may use 93 for an A, 70 for a distinction, or a completely different scale. Type the percentage your own handbook uses. Do not treat those four buttons as an official scale.

When this is the wrong question

A UK classification is usually built from module marks and year weights, not from one course final. Use the UK degree classification calculator with the weights in your handbook. The guide to marks, percentages and grades explains why a percentage in one system is not a grade in another. Many Pakistani universities report GPA and CGPA on a scale the institution chooses. HEC guidance does not authorise you to invent a conversion: use the points on your transcript, or the formula your university publishes. BISE-style marks convert with obtained ÷ total × 100, which is a different sum from a weighted final. If a target is unreachable, re-read the weight. Students often type 40 when the final is 40 marks on a paper, not 40% of the course.

How to use the number

Once you have a required score, compare it with a recent practice paper instead of treating it as a mood. If you need 55% and recent papers are near that, the plan is to hold the standard. If you need a score your papers have never approached, the gap is the useful fact: the calculator only rearranges the three numbers you typed. Keep those inputs written down so you can recalculate if the weight changes. Nothing you type is stored or uploaded.

If the course is still in raw marks, convert them to a percentage before you use the final-grade formula. Mixing “42 out of 50” with “weight 30%” without converting produces a precise-looking wrong answer. Use the percentage calculator for that step, then come back with percentages only.

Frequently asked questions

What information do I need before I calculate a final?

You need three numbers from the syllabus or gradebook: your current percentage with the final left out, the final’s weight as a percentage of the course, and the overall percentage you are aiming for. If any of those is missing, the result is a guess.

What if the score I need is over 100%?

The target is out of reach if the final is marked out of 100. The calculator then shows the best course grade a perfect final could produce. Lower the target or check whether extra credit exists. Do not assume a mark above 100 is allowed.

Does this formula work for UK degree classes or a Pakistani CGPA?

Only when the final really is one weighted percentage inside a course that is already in percent. UK classifications usually combine module marks and year weights, and many Pakistani universities report GPA or CGPA on a scale the institution chooses. Use the matching calculator and your own rules.
